          Triage: TST and MITT

          Since April 2023, NHS England has used two tools. The Ten Second Triage tool (TST) is for anyone at the scene. The NHS Major Incident Triage Tool (MITT) is the clinical triage tool for NHS responders. Together they replaced the Triage Sieve, the Triage Sort and the Paediatric Triage Tape, with full NHS implementation due by 30 June 2024.

          Ten Second Triage (TST)

          NHS England / NARU, version 1.0, April 2023. Uses no physiological measurements.

          1. Walking?Uninjured: direct to a place of relative safety. Injured walking: casualty collection point.
            Yes: P3
          2. Severe bleeding?
            Yes: Pressure / Tourniquet / Packing, then P1
          3. Talking?If yes, make a deliberate search for penetrating injury, front, back and sides: neck, chest, armpits, back, abdomen, groin and buttocks.
            Yes: penetrating injury found P1
            none found P2
          4. Breathing?Not talking: open the airway if able, then a 10-second breathing check (look, listen, feel).
            Yes: recovery position, P1
            No: recovery position, NOT BREATHING
            CPR if resources allow. It must not take priority over stopping external bleeding and opening airways.
          Additional rule: any child under 2 is automatically P1

          Bands

          P1 white on red   P2 black on yellow   P3 white on green   NOT BREATHING black on silver. A white checked border identifies TST bands and separates them from MITT cards.

          No DEAD category. "Not Breathing" records the objective finding of the breathing check. Later, a healthcare practitioner with time reassesses the casualty as a viable resuscitation (P1) or ROLE/PLE (dead).

          NHS Major Incident Triage Tool (MITT)

          NHS England, April 2023; Vassallo et al., EMJ 2022. Same thresholds for adults and children.

          1. Catastrophic bleeding?
            Yes: P1
          2. Walking?
            Yes: P3
          3. Not breathing after airway opening?In children under 12 after submersion, immersion or smoke inhalation, consider 5 rescue breaths.
            Yes: DEAD
          4. No response to voice?
            Yes: recovery position, P1
          5. Aged under 2?
            Yes: P1
          6. Respiratory rate <12 or ≥24?i.e. outside 12–23
            Yes: P1
          7. Heart rate ≥100?The published tool reads "100 or more". The EMJ 2022 text reads ">100". Check your local card.
            Yes: P1
          Otherwise: P2

          MITT keeps a DEAD category. It has no "Pit Stop" pathway. The Survivor Reception Centre route for the uninjured has gone: every living person involved is at least P3.

          Hospital re-triage on arrival

          A scene band is a starting point, not a final category. The draft SOP 1 asks the ED to re-triage every arrival with MITT at a single triage point, including those tagged at the scene with TST and those who arrive by themselves. Check your trust's major incident plan for the local process.

          • Self-presenters are common. At Manchester Arena, 95 of the 153 patients who reached hospital made their own way there as P3, and 14 went to hospitals outside the local network (Dark et al., EMJ 2021).
          • Walking at the scene does not mean well now. TST stops at step 1 for anyone walking. Re-triage P3 patients at intervals and whenever there is concern. Drill cards SIM_02 and SIM_07 practise this.
          • No one leaves as "uninjured". Every living person is at least P3 under MITT.
          • Record RR, HR and the MITT category for every patient, including children. In TARN data, 67.2% of under-16s lacked complete pre-hospital physiology, and those missing it were younger and had higher mortality (Chernbumroong et al., preprint).
          • A silver Not Breathing band is an observation, not a declaration of death. A clinician reassesses for resuscitation or recognition of life extinct (NHS England letter, April 2023).

          Hospital SOPs (draft)

          Draft SOPs for a receiving hospital's emergency department during a mass casualty incident. They are taken from the "Mass casualty SOPs (draft)" document dated 16 October 2026, and they use only content confirmed in the accuracy review.

          Draft. Not for use until signed off by your trust's clinical governance, pharmacy and emergency preparedness leads. Text in [square brackets] is for local detail. These SOPs cover the hospital only, not ambulance, fire or police scene work.

          SOP 1: Triage and re-triage at the front door

          Trigger: major incident declared, or casualties arriving from an incident before declaration.

          Why: at Manchester Arena, 95 of the 153 patients who reached hospital made their own way there, bypassing ambulance triage, and 14 went to hospitals outside the local network (EMJ 2021). ED vital signs were largely unrecorded, so triage could not be reviewed afterwards.

          Actions

          1. Open a single triage point at the ED entrance, staffed by a senior clinician and a scribe. Divert all arrivals, ambulance and self-presenting, through it.
          2. Treat any casualty still bleeding catastrophically first: pressure, tourniquet or packing, then P1.
          3. Triage every arrival with the NHS Major Incident Triage Tool (MITT), including those already tagged at scene with Ten Second Triage (TST). A scene band is a starting point, not a final category.
          4. Apply MITT in order:
            1. Catastrophic bleeding: P1
            2. Walking: P3
            3. Not breathing after airway opening: DEAD on MITT. In children under 12 after submersion, immersion or smoke inhalation, consider 5 rescue breaths first.
            4. Does not respond to voice: recovery position, P1
            5. Aged under 2: P1
            6. Respiratory rate below 12 or 24 and above: P1
            7. Heart rate 100 or above: P1 [confirm against current NHS England MITT card; the EMJ 2022 text reads "above 100"]
            8. Otherwise: P2
          5. Do not discharge or send anyone home as "uninjured" from the triage point. Every living person involved is at least P3, because the Survivor Reception Centre category has been removed from MITT (EMJ 2022).
          6. Record respiratory rate, heart rate and the MITT category on every patient, including children. In the TARN paediatric data, two thirds of under-16s had incomplete pre-hospital physiology, and those missing it were younger and had higher mortality (medRxiv 2021, preprint).
          7. Re-triage everyone in the P3 area at fixed intervals [local interval] and whenever a patient or relative raises concern. Move anyone who now meets a P1 or P2 criterion immediately.
          8. Hand the incident number, MITT category and time to the patient tracking officer [role] for every patient.

          TST bands you may see on arrival: red P1, yellow P2, green P3, and black on silver "Not Breathing". TST bands have a white checked border. A Not Breathing band is an observation, not a declaration of death: a clinician must reassess for resuscitation or recognition of life extinct (NHS England, April 2023).

          SOP 2: Surge and patient flow in the first six hours

          Trigger: major incident standby or declared.

          Why: at Manchester Arena, 138 of 153 hospital patients (90%) arrived within 6 hours across 11 hospitals. Of the 153, 24 (16%) needed critical care and 21 (14%) needed emergency damage control surgery; only 5 needed secondary transfer in the first 24 hours (EMJ 2021). Plan for the bulk of demand early, and for children: 44 of the 153 were under 16.

          Actions

          1. Hospital command [role] opens the major incident plan and confirms the hospital's role in the network: MTC, trauma unit or local emergency hospital.
          2. Clear the ED: move patients who can safely go to wards, assessment units or discharge, so resuscitation bays and majors cubicles are free before the first arrivals.
          3. Set up separate P1, P2 and P3 areas with a named lead for each, plus a paediatric area if children are likely [location].
          4. Alert theatres, critical care, blood bank, radiology and paediatrics. Stand down elective theatre lists as directed by command.
          5. Agree critical care surge capacity with the critical care lead. As one network's example, the Severn guideline asks units to double Level 3 ventilated capacity and hold it for at least 96 hours (STAG). Insert your own network's figure.
          6. Agree with the network a P1 receiving capacity for this hospital [local figure]. No validated national hourly cap was found; do not use the "8 P1 per hour per MTC" figure from the original report.
          7. Report capacity to the network and ambulance service at set intervals [interval], so dispersal can be adjusted.
          8. Plan staff rotation from the start and keep a reserve. Arrange psychological support for staff after stand-down; inadequate psychological support was a reported gap after the 2017 attacks (Skryabina et al.).
          SOP 3: Massive haemorrhage activation in a mass casualty incident

          Trigger: any casualty with suspected major haemorrhage, typically penetrating torso or junctional wounds, blast fragment injuries or traumatic amputation.

          Why: penetrating and blast wounds dominated the survivors reaching hospital after the 2017 attacks. Unverified (this summary is the draft's own; the verification tables confirm limb and torso injuries predominated at Manchester and stab injuries predominated at London Bridge, but not this wider statement). Several patients bleeding at once can exhaust blood bank stock quickly, so blood bank must know the scale early.

          Actions

          1. Declare a major haemorrhage using your trust's call [local name, e.g. "Code Red"] and give the patient's major incident number, not only their name.
          2. Blood bank lead [role] is told at declaration how many P1 casualties are expected, so they can call in emergency stock from NHS Blood and Transplant early.
          3. Give tranexamic acid as per local protocol [dose and timing]. The London Code Red algorithm (2018) uses 1 g over 10 minutes.Check JRCALC / TOXBASE / BNF and local formulary. Not verified against JRCALC or TOXBASE.
          4. Issue packs as per local protocol [pack names and contents]. For reference, the London Code Red algorithm (2018) uses:
            PackContents
            Pack A4 units red cells and 4 units FFP
            Pack B6 units red cells, 6 units FFP, 2 pools cryoprecipitate, 1 pool platelets

            Source: London Code Red algorithm 2018. Other networks use different packs; replace with your own.

          5. Send near-patient and laboratory coagulation tests as per local protocol and use them to guide later packs.
          6. Stand down the major haemorrhage call for each patient as soon as bleeding is controlled, so stock goes to the next patient.
          7. Blood bank reports remaining stock to hospital command at set intervals [interval].
          SOP 4: Smoke inhalation, carbon monoxide and cyanide

          Trigger: casualties from an enclosed-space or high-rise fire, or any casualty with smoke exposure.

          Why: fire gases cause carbon monoxide and cyanide poisoning alongside airway burns. Standard pulse oximeters cannot tell carboxyhaemoglobin from oxyhaemoglobin, so a normal SpO2 does not exclude CO poisoning (FPHC/BBA 2020).

          Actions

          1. Give high-flow oxygen via a non-rebreathing reservoir mask at 10–15 L/min to every smoke-exposed casualty.
          2. Do not rely on SpO2. Measure COHb by co-oximetry or blood gas. A level above 30% indicates severe poisoning. In confirmed CO poisoning, continue 100% oxygen until COHb is normal (below 3%). Without CO poisoning, aim for SpO2 94–98%.
          3. Give a cyanide antidote to any smoke-exposed casualty with altered mental status and/or cardiovascular instability. Do not wait for lactate or cyanide levels.
            • Hydroxocobalamin, adults: 5 g IV over 15 minutes. A second 5 g may follow; maximum total 10 g.
            • Hydroxocobalamin, children (0–18 years): 70 mg/kg, not exceeding 5 g; maximum total 140 mg/kg, not exceeding 10 g.
            • Source: Cyanokit SmPC. The FPHC/BBA body text gives 100 mg/kg, which conflicts with its own summary table and the licence; do not use it.
            Check JRCALC / TOXBASE / BNF and local formulary. Not verified against JRCALC or TOXBASE.
          4. Assess the airway early. Consider prophylactic large-bore tracheal intubation for suspected impending airway compromise: full-thickness facial burns, stridor, respiratory distress, swelling on laryngoscopy, smoke inhalation or singed nasal hairs. The most experienced airway operator available should do it.
          5. If a surgical airway is needed through burnt neck tissue: make a longitudinal skin incision to expose the anatomy, then a transverse incision into the identified cricothyroid membrane.
          6. Record where hydroxocobalamin has been given: it colours skin and urine red and can interfere with some laboratory tests. Unverified (the draft notes this is from general knowledge and was not checked; confirm with the SmPC and local laboratory)

          Stock: confirm where hydroxocobalamin is held and how many doses are available [location, number], and how to get more from the network.

          SOP 5: Burns in a mass casualty incident

          Trigger: two or more burns casualties, or any burns casualty during a declared major incident.

          Why: all of these steps come from the FPHC/BBA pre-hospital burns consensus (version 2, April 2020), which is graded C/D expert opinion. They are recommendations, not mandates (FPHC/BBA 2020).

          Actions

          1. Manage airway and smoke inhalation first (SOP 4).
          2. Cool the burn with cool running water for 20 minutes. This is ideally started within 10 minutes and is still worthwhile up to 3 hours after injury. Use water below 20°C; do not use ice water.
          3. Use hydrogel dressings only if running water is not available.
          4. Estimate burn size with a Lund and Browder chart and record it in one of three bands: below 20%, 20–50%, or above 50% TBSA.
          5. Start warmed fluid resuscitation for adults and children with burns above 20% TBSA, calculated by weight [local formula]. For children, use 0.9% sodium chloride if available.Check JRCALC / TOXBASE / BNF and local formulary. Not verified against JRCALC or TOXBASE.
          6. Prevent hypothermia: active warming and regular temperature checks, during cooling as well as after.
          7. Calculate the revised Baux score (age + %TBSA + 17 if inhalation injury) to support decisions. It predicts probability of death, not need for intensive care (Osler 2010).
          8. Contact the burns network [Burn Care Operational Delivery Network contact] early for advice, bed availability and onward transfer.

          Note: "Burn Rapid Response Team" and "Burn Specialist Team" in the original report are WHO Emergency Medical Team terms, not UK NHS structures. Use your burns network's own arrangements.

          SOP 6: Suspected nerve agent casualties

          Trigger: unexplained collapse with pinpoint pupils, heavy secretions or breathing difficulty, especially in more than one person, or any notice from police or ambulance of a chemical incident.

          Why: at Salisbury and Amesbury in 2018, five people were poisoned with a Novichok agent and one died. Novichok agents inhibit acetylcholinesterase, and the inhibited enzyme "ages" within minutes, so oximes work poorly (Noga and Jurowski 2022). Atropine titrated to effect is the mainstay. The actions below follow the PHE/NHS England ED briefing on suspected Novichok poisoning (July 2018). It is from 2018: check TOXBASE for any newer guidance before sign-off.

          Actions

          1. Protect staff first. Put on the PPE your CBRN plan specifies [local PPE] before touching the casualty. Isolate the casualty and anyone who came with them.
          2. Decontaminate: remove the casualty's clothing and double-bag it. Wash skin with soap or detergent and water where time allows. Follow your trust's decontamination plan [location of decontamination area].
          3. Inform hospital command, the police and UKHSA, and contact TOXBASE or the National Poisons Information Service [contact].
          4. Consider opiate overdose first: it is the most important differential diagnosis. Give a trial of naloxone.
          5. Give atropine IV, IM or IO:
            • Adults and children aged 12 or over: 4.0–4.2 mg (8 × 500 microgram or 7 × 600 microgram ampoules).
            • Children under 12: 50–75 microgram/kg.
            • Repeat every 5 minutes until secretions are minimal and the patient is atropinised: chest clear, heart rate above 80 per minute, adequate blood pressure.
            • There is no maximum dose. Do not stop at a fixed total.
            • Do not use pupil size as an endpoint.
            Check JRCALC / TOXBASE / BNF and local formulary. Not verified against JRCALC or TOXBASE.
          6. Give oximes and benzodiazepines as advised by TOXBASE or NPIS [doses to be added by pharmacy]. Current therapy for nerve agents combines atropine, oximes and benzodiazepines with supportive care including ventilation (Thiermann et al. 2025).
          7. Secure the airway and ventilate as needed. Plan for prolonged critical care: Salisbury District Hospital's ICU cared for five critically ill patients (Haslam et al., BJA 2022).
          8. Do not give magnesium sulfate or intravenous lipid emulsion on the strength of the original report. No source supports either for nerve agents.

          Stock: confirm how much atropine the hospital holds and where [location, number of ampoules]. Repeated doses at 4 mg each use ampoules quickly, so agree how to get more.

          First crew on scene

          From the 2018 WMAS card, which refers crews to the major incident action cards carried in the vehicle. Follow your own service's action cards.

          Driver

          • Park as near to the scene as safety permits. Leave roof beacons on.
          • Send a windscreen report: press priority on the radio, give call sign, say what you see, follow up with METHANE.
          • The vehicle becomes the ambulance command point, within easy reach of police and fire control units, until relieved.
          • Switch to the designated talk group when instructed.
          • Stay with the vehicle and keep a communications link between the scene and ambulance control.

          Attendant

          • Assume the role of ambulance incident commander until relieved by a suitably trained officer.
          • Carry out a rapid assessment and send an initial METHANE report; send updated METHANE reports.
          • Liaise with other emergency services if present.
          • With other services, identify the multi-agency rendezvous point, access and egress, the ambulance parking point and the casualty clearing point.

          STEP 1-2-3 Plus

          Safety triggers for responders.

          1. STEP 1One person incapacitated with no obvious reason
            Approach using standard protocols
          2. STEP 2Two people incapacitated with no obvious reason
            Approach with caution using standard protocols
          3. STEP 3Three or more people in close proximity, incapacitated with no obvious reason
            Use caution and follow Plus: the CBRN first responder flowchart

          Remove-Remove-Remove

          1. Remove the patient from the immediate area to avoid further exposure.
          2. Remove outer clothing if affected.
          3. Remove the substance from skin: dry decontamination unless the skin is itchy, burning or painful.

          In hospital, the PHE/NHS England nerve agent briefing describes primary decontamination as removing and double-bagging clothing, and washing skin with soap or detergent and water where time allows (see SOP 6).

          CBRN first responder flowchart (summary)

          1. Hazard assessment. Report arrival and location to control. Identify hazards and give a situation report. Apply STEP 1-2-3 Plus.
          2. Are there 3 or more casualties in close proximity with no obvious reason? No: follow STEP 1 or 2. Yes: continue.
          3. Jointly agree hazards (joint dynamic hazard assessment) and work with other agencies. Control the scene; identify safe arrival routes, the RVP and additional resources; direct walking casualties to a place of relative safety (warm zone).
          4. Are there casualties unable to walk who need rescue from the hazard area? If yes, each agency undertakes its specific risk assessment. If benefits outweigh risks, the fire and rescue service undertakes rescue with minimum personnel in the most appropriate PPE/RPE, informed by the joint assessment. If risks outweigh benefits, apply control measures.
          5. Evacuate, communicate and advise. Disrobe. Decontaminate. Communicate with the public and multi-agency responders throughout.

          Responder experience: what the research says

          Stephens et al. (BMJ Open 2026) interviewed 26 armed and unarmed police, paramedics, doctors, fire officers and survivors from 7/7, Westminster Bridge, London Bridge/Borough Market, Manchester Arena and Fishmongers' Hall.

          • Unarmed police were willing to help, but training and first-aid kits did not match: "I just felt really, really under-prepared".
          • Armed officers described "a moral challenge of stepping over injured" until the threat was neutralised.
          • Responders lacked shared mental models of what was "unsafe" because of zoning. One prehospital physician described the alternative to following armed officers in as going "into that clipboard zone and start having meetings", meaning a retreat into process.

          The Manchester Arena Inquiry Volume 2 noted a "paucity of analgesia provision within the inner cordon" and proposed fentanyl lozenges or sufentanil for HART (as reported by Paramedic Practice).

              Ten Second Triage (TST)

              Anyone at the scene · NHS England / NARU v1.0, April 2023 · No physiological measurements

              1. Walking? Yes: P3 (uninjured: place of relative safety; injured walking: casualty collection point)
              2. Severe bleeding? Yes: Pressure / Tourniquet / Packing, then P1
              3. Talking? Yes: search for penetrating injury, front, back and sides: neck, chest, armpits, back, abdomen, groin, buttocks. Found P1; none P2. No: go to 4.
              4. Breathing? Open airway if able; 10-second look, listen, feel. Yes: recovery position, P1. No: recovery position, NOT BREATHING. CPR if resources allow; it must not detract from stopping bleeding and opening airways.

              Any child under 2: automatically P1. No DEAD category. TST bands have a white checked border.

              Training aid from the Mass Casualty Handbook. Check against the current official card. Source: NARU TST supplementary information v1.0; NHS England TST tool, April 2023.

              NHS Major Incident Triage Tool (MITT)

              NHS responders · NHS England, April 2023 · Same thresholds at all ages

              1. Catastrophic bleeding? P1
              2. Walking? P3
              3. Breathing? If not, open airway. Still not breathing: DEAD. Children under 12 after submersion, immersion or smoke: consider 5 rescue breaths.
              4. Responds to voice? If not: recovery position, P1
              5. Aged under 2? P1
              6. Breathing rate outside 12–23? P1
              7. Heart rate 100 or more? P1 (EMJ 2022 text reads ">100": check your local card)
              8. Otherwise P2

              No "Pit Stop" pathway. Every living person involved is at least P3.

              Training aid from the Mass Casualty Handbook. Check against the current official card. Source: NHS England MITT, April 2023; Vassallo et al. EMJ 2022.
