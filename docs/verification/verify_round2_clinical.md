# Verification round 2 — clinical and triage claims

Date of check: 30 October 2026. Method: every "confirmed" or "partly" verdict rests on a page actually opened in this session; wording in quotation marks is verbatim from that page. Where the page could not be opened (NICE, PubMed/PMC, Europe PMC and web.archive.org were all blocked from this environment), that is stated and a secondary copy is cited with reduced confidence. British English throughout.

Verdict key: **Confirmed** / **Partly** / **Contradicted** / **Not found**.

## 1. MITT heart-rate wording: "100 or more" (tool) vs ">100" (EMJ)

| Item | Finding |
|---|---|
| Claim | The NHS England MITT card reads "100 or more" while Vassallo et al. EMJ 2022 reads ">100"; need the exact wording in each, plus the breathing-rate thresholds. |
| Verdict | **Confirmed — the two sources do differ at the boundary value, and neither quotes "≥100".** |
| Exact finding | **NHS England MITT PDF (April 2023, PRN00365ii):** text strings in document order include "Aged Over" / "2 Years", "Breathing Rate" / "12 - 23", "Heart Rate" / "100 or More". The digits "24" do not appear anywhere on the card; nor does "MPTT". The card expresses the respiratory criterion as the *normal* band "12 - 23" (outside it → P1), not as "<12 or ≥24". **NHS England MITT long-read (text version):** "Breathing rate 12 – 23 – No = P1"; "Heart rate – No = P2" — the text version gives **no numeric heart-rate threshold at all** (the number is only on the card graphic). **EMJ 2022 (Vassallo, Moran, Cowburn, Smith, "New NHS Prehospital Major Incident Triage Tool: from MIMMS to MITT", EMJ 2022;39(11):800–802):** body paragraph under "Physiological thresholds": "The new thresholds (Heart Rate >100 and Respiratory Rate <12 or ≥ 24) ... incorporate the pulse and respiratory rate thresholds from the Modified Physiological Triage Tool, MPTT-24." The symbol is ">" with no space, in body text, not "≥". **NARU TST Supplementary Information v1.0:** contains no MITT heart- or breathing-rate statement. **NHS England letter/long-read "Major incident triage tools"** (PRN00358): "MITT has been derived from the Modified Physiological Triage Tool-24 (MPTT)"; no thresholds given. The MPTT-24 implementation paper (Vassallo, Smith, Wallis, J R Army Med Corps 2017, doi:10.1136/jramc-2017-000819) states "increasing the upper respiratory rate threshold to 24 breaths per minute (MPTT-24)" but its heart-rate threshold is only in Figure 2 (image), so the original MPTT-24 pulse boundary could not be read in this session. |
| Interpretation | The tool treats HR = 100 as abnormal (P1); the EMJ text, read literally, does not. Respiratory rate is consistent between them (12–23 normal ⇔ <12 or ≥24 abnormal). No source quotes "≥100". For teaching, the **published tool** governs. |
| Sources | https://www.england.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/PRN00365ii-nhs-major-incident-triage-tool-april-2023.pdf ; https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/nhs-major-incident-triage-tool-mitt/ ; https://emj.bmj.com/content/39/11/800 ; https://www.england.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/naru-tst-non-clinical-responders-supplementary-information.pdf ; https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/major-incident-triage-tools-2/ ; https://militaryhealth.bmj.com/content/jramc/early/2017/12/04/jramc-2017-000819.full.pdf |
| Confidence | High for the tool PDF, long-read and EMJ wording (all opened directly; EMJ text re-checked twice). Medium that the EMJ full PDF contains no table giving "≥100" (the .full.pdf would not load; the HTML article text was used). |
| Suggested site wording | "MITT (NHS England, April 2023) assigns P1 if the breathing rate is outside 12–23/min or the heart rate is **100 or more**. The derivation paper (Vassallo et al., EMJ 2022) describes the MPTT-24 thresholds as 'Heart Rate >100 and Respiratory Rate <12 or ≥24'; the published card, which is the operational standard, counts a heart rate of exactly 100 as abnormal." |

## 2. Hydroxocobalamin (Cyanokit) SmPC: red colouration and laboratory/dialysis interference

| Item | Finding |
|---|---|
| Claim | SmPC states red colouring of skin, mucous membranes and urine; interference with laboratory tests (which) and with haemodialysis / co-oximetry. |
| Verdict | **Partly — all confirmed except co-oximetry, which the SmPC does not mention.** |
| Exact finding | **§4.4:** "Because of its deep red colour, hydroxocobalamin has the potential to induce a red colouration of the skin and therefore may interfere with burn assessment." "...hydroxocobalamin has the potential to interfere with determination of laboratory parameters (e.g. clinical chemistry, haematology, coagulation, and urine parameters)." Table "Observed in vitro interferences of hydroxocobalamin with laboratory tests" (after a 5 g dose): *No interference* — calcium, sodium, potassium, chloride, urea, GGT, erythrocytes, haematocrit, MCV, leucocytes, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, neutrophils, platelets. *Artificially increased* — creatinine, total and conjugated bilirubin, triglycerides, cholesterol, total protein, glucose, albumin, alkaline phosphatase, haemoglobin, MCH, MCHC. *Artificially decreased* — ALT, amylase. *Unpredictable* — phosphate, uric acid, AST, CK, CK-MB, LDH, aPTT, PT/INR. Duration: clinical chemistry "24 hours with the exception of bilirubin (up to 4 days)"; haematology "12-16 hours"; coagulation "24 hours"; "Interference following a 10 g dose can be expected to last up to an additional 24 hours." "Hydroxocobalamin may interfere with all urine colorimetric parameters. The effects on these tests typically last 48 hours after a 5 g dose, but may persist for longer periods." **Haemodialysis (§4.4):** "Because of its deep red color, hydroxocobalamin may cause haemodialysis machines to shut down due to an erroneous detection of a 'blood leak'. This should be considered before haemodialysis is initiated in patients treated with hydroxocobalamin." **§4.8:** "Reversible red colouration of the skin and mucous membranes: most patients will experience it up to 15 days after administration of Cyanokit." "Chromaturia: all patients will show a dark red colouration of the urine quite marked during the first three days following administration. Urine colouration may last up to 35 days after administration of Cyanokit." "Cyanokit may cause red discolouration of the plasma, which may cause artificial elevation or reduction in the levels of certain laboratory parameters." **Co-oximetry:** the word does not appear anywhere in the SmPC. |
| Source | https://www.medicines.org.uk/emc/product/4786/smpc |
| Confidence | High. |
| Suggested site wording | "Hydroxocobalamin turns the skin and mucous membranes red (reversible; up to about 15 days) and the urine dark red (marked for 3 days; up to 35 days). Its colour interferes with many laboratory assays — e.g. falsely raised creatinine, bilirubin, glucose, total protein, haemoglobin and MCHC; falsely low ALT and amylase; unpredictable AST, CK, LDH, aPTT and PT/INR — for roughly 24 h after 5 g (bilirubin up to 4 days), and with all urine colorimetric tests for about 48 h. Take baseline bloods before the dose. It can also trigger the 'blood leak' alarm and shut down haemodialysis machines (Cyanokit SmPC §4.4, §4.8)." Remove any reference to co-oximetry unless a separate source is cited. |

## 3. Hypothermia "independently associated with mortality" in burns

| Item | Finding |
|---|---|
| Claim | Hypothermia is independently associated with mortality in burns. |
| Verdict | **Confirmed.** |
| Exact finding | **Hostler D, Weaver MD, Ziembicki JA, et al. "Admission temperature and survival in patients admitted to burn centers." J Burn Care Res 2013;34(5):498–506 (doi:10.1097/BCR.0b013e3182a231fb).** Statewide (Pennsylvania) trauma registry, 2000–2011, patients treated at accredited burn centres; "Primary burn injury was coded 17,098 times during the study period", "3809 were not treated at a burn center and 1192 were excluded for missing data". Hypothermia defined as "T ≤36.5 C". Result: "Admission hypothermia was independently associated with mortality (1.91 [1.58-2.29])" — adjusted odds ratio from hierarchical multivariable logistic regression, adjusted for age, sex, burn surface area, comorbidities, injury severity, transport mode, temperature-measurement method, year and hospital. Conclusion: "Hypothermia at hospital admission is independently associated with mortality in burn patients." Note: the threshold is mild (≤36.5 °C), and this is an association in registry data, not proof of causation. **The FPHC/BBA consensus itself** (Temperature Management, cited as Level III evidence) states: "Hypothermia in burn patients has been shown to be independently associated with mortality", with the Grade C recommendation "Avoid hypothermia through active warming measures and regular body temperature monitoring." A second study ("Early hypothermia as risk factor in severely burned patients: a retrospective outcome study", Burns 2019, PMID 31378620) and the ABA-registry analysis ("Temperature derangement on admission predicts mortality in burn patients", PMC10185263) were identified but PubMed/PMC/Europe PMC pages could not be opened from this environment, so they are not cited. |
| Sources | https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/admission-temperature-and-survival-in-patients-admitted-to-burn-c/ ; https://fphc.rcsed.ac.uk/media/2957/2020-09-20-burns-consensus.pdf |
| Confidence | High for the Hostler abstract (institutional repository copy of the published abstract) and the consensus wording; medium that the full paper's numbers match the abstract (full text not opened). |
| Suggested site wording | "Hypothermia (admission temperature ≤36.5 °C) was independently associated with death in a US statewide registry of burn-centre patients (adjusted OR 1.91, 95% CI 1.58–2.29; Hostler et al., J Burn Care Res 2013). The FPHC/BBA consensus (2020) therefore recommends active warming and regular temperature monitoring (Grade C)." |

## 4. Atropine "for days or weeks" in nerve-agent / organophosphate poisoning

| Item | Finding |
|---|---|
| Claim | Atropine infusions may be needed "for days or weeks" after nerve-agent/OP poisoning. |
| Verdict | **Partly — supported for OP *insecticide* poisoning (days; prolonged cases reported); not supported for nerve agents by any UK or US guidance opened, which describe hours rather than days; "weeks" was not found in any guidance.** |
| Exact finding | **UKHSA, Compendium of chemical hazards: Nerve agents (Incident Management), first published Nov 2017, full update Sept 2024:** gives no atropine dose, infusion or duration; "The use of atropine and pralidoxime is recommended for patients with excessive secretions"; refers readers to TOXBASE. Notes "Miosis ... may be painful and last for several days" and "The intermediate syndrome (delayed respiratory failure after apparent resolution of cholinergic symptoms) has not been recorded after nerve agent poisoning." **PHE, CBRN incidents: clinical management and health protection, 2nd ed. 2018 (Gent & Milton):** atropine 4.0–4.2 mg (adults/≥12 y), 50–75 microgram/kg (<12 y), "Doses repeated every five minutes until secretions are minimal and the patient is 'atropinised'"; **no atropine infusion or duration is stated**; pralidoxime "Continuation treatment: infusion at 8mg/kg/hour ... continue the infusion for 12 to 24 hours; on withdrawal, if there is deterioration, restart the pralidoxime infusion." **PHE/NHSE ED briefing note (2018)** — same dosing, no infusion (read in round 1). **US HHS CHEMM, "Nerve Agents Hospital Management":** "atropine has not been required for more than 3 hours to treat the life threatening effects"; non-life-threatening effects "have required atropine for 6-36 hours"; OP ingestion by contrast has needed "hundreds of mgs a day of atropine"; after atropinisation "start an infusion of atropine giving per hour around 10-20 % of the dose used to initially atropinize the patient"; "Continue the oxime infusion until atropine has not been required for 12-24 hrs". **Thiermann, Aurbek, Worek, "Treatment of Nerve Agent Poisoning", in Chemical Warfare Toxicology Vol 2 (RSC, 2016):** "In severe cases, atropine therapy should be maintained by continuous infusion"; post-acute phase "0.5–1 mg h−1 should be sufficient"; "Long term therapy will only be necessary in severely poisoned victims or in patients developing complications"; no days/weeks figure. **OP insecticide literature:** Park et al., J Korean Soc Clin Toxicol 2020;18(2):123–129: "high dose of atropine should be infused for several days under certain controlled conditions" (citing Eddleston & Clark, Greene, Howland, Eddleston 2019); in their series median atropine infusion "5.0 days (3.0-7.0)". A case report titled "Severe acute organophosphate poisoning managed with 2-month prolonged atropine therapy" (Ann Med Surg 2023, PMC10553172) exists but the page could not be opened. Eddleston et al., Lancet 2008;371:597–607 abstract: "we still do not know how the core treatments—atropine, oximes, and diazepam—should best be given" (no duration in abstract). Hulse et al., AJRCCM 2014;190:1342–54 abstract: insecticides cause "a delayed intermediate syndrome that requires prolonged ventilation" (no atropine duration). WHO guidance was not opened. |
| Sources | https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/670fd675e84ae1fd8592f503/Nerve_Agents_UKHSA_IM_.pdf ; https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5b10120c40f0b634d14c21ed/Chemical_biological_radiological_and_nuclear_incidents_clinical_management_and_health_protection.pdf ; https://chemm.hhs.gov/na_hospital_mmg.htm ; https://books.rsc.org/books/edited-volume/656/chapter/350941/Treatment-of-Nerve-Agent-Poisoning ; https://koreascience.or.kr/article/JAKO202007650437659.pdf ; https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/management-of-acute-organophosphorus-pesticide-poisoning/ ; https://eprints.ncl.ac.uk/209676 |
| Confidence | High that UK guidance states no infusion duration; high for the CHEMM and RSC wording; medium for the OP-insecticide "several days" statement (one Korean journal paper, citing standard reviews not opened). |
| Suggested site wording | "UK guidance (PHE 2018; UKHSA 2024) titrates atropine in repeated boluses every 5 minutes to the endpoint of minimal secretions, clear chest, heart rate >80/min and adequate blood pressure, and gives no fixed duration. In nerve-agent vapour exposure the atropine requirement is usually measured in hours (CHEMM: life-threatening effects rarely beyond 3 h; lesser effects 6–36 h). Severe cases may need a maintenance infusion (Thiermann et al. 2016). Prolonged, multi-day atropine infusions are described mainly in organophosphate *insecticide* poisoning (median about 5 days in one series), and exceptional cases lasting weeks have been reported. Salisbury/Amesbury patients required weeks of intensive care, but no published UK source specifies their atropine duration." Drop the unqualified phrase "for days or weeks". |
