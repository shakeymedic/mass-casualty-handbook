# Verification table: European (non-UK) terrorist mass casualty incidents

Compiled 30 October 2026 for the UK ED teaching site. Every figure below comes from a source opened in this session, through PubMed records (abstract or PMC full text) or web pages fetched directly. If a figure could not be sourced, it is marked **not found**. Where sources disagree, both figures are given. Headline figures taken from news outlets are labelled as such.

Method notes:
- Most peer-reviewed figures come from PubMed abstracts. Full text was read only for Buschmann 2020 (Berlin, PMC7449987). Hirsch 2015 (Lancet) was read from a full-text copy hosted by the University of Rochester (URMC). The two *Emergencias* papers (Barcelona) were read as PDFs from the journal site.
- Several pages could not be fetched: thelancet.com, link.springer.com, ResearchGate and the Belgian Chamber PDFs (blocked or rejected). Nothing from those pages is used.
- Confidence: **high** means a peer-reviewed or official source reporting the figure directly. **Medium** means a single source, a trade or medical-chamber publication, or a figure with internal inconsistency. **Low** means a news outlet only, or unresolved disagreement.

## Cross-cutting teaching points (each explicitly supported by the cited source)

1. **Firearm attacks consume more surgical resource than blast.** In Paris, 54% of admitted casualties needed emergency surgery, more often for gunshot than explosion injuries (57% vs 35%). Orthopaedic surgery was the commonest type, and vascular surgery was needed fastest (median 53 min). (P-ICM: https://doi.org/10.1007/s00134-019-05724-9)
2. **Expect prehospital undertriage, so re-triage at the front door.** Paris prehospital triage undertriaged 36% of casualties and overtriaged 8%. (P-TRI: https://doi.org/10.1097/MEJ.0000000000000771)
3. **Closest-hospital overload and overtriage are predictable.** In Madrid almost 60% of casualties went to the two nearest hospitals, with overtriage and uneven distribution. (M-EJT: https://doi.org/10.1007/s00068-008-8805-2; M-CCM: https://doi.org/10.1097/01.ccm.0000151072.17826.72)
4. **Blast injury pattern.** Most blast deaths are immediate (93% in Madrid). About half of survivors had tympanic membrane perforation. Blast lung was common in critical patients, with 88% survival. Ear-lobe amputation and upper thoracic spine fractures marked critical injury. (M-WJS: https://doi.org/10.1007/s00268-008-9557-1)
5. **Protect the trauma centre from walking wounded and use surgical command.** Oslo's trauma centre treated 31 patients, 20 of them with ISS>15, with 5% critical mortality. Surge capacity was never exceeded, which the authors linked to dual surgical command and being protected from large numbers of walking wounded. (O-TC: https://doi.org/10.1097/TA.0b013e31825a787f)
6. **Move to minimum acceptable care in imaging.** In Oslo, CT in the ED was restricted to suspected severe head injury during the surge. (O-RAD: https://doi.org/10.1007/s00330-016-4677-8)
7. **Vehicle ramming produces road-traffic-type blunt polytrauma.** In Nice, 25% of fractures were open. In Berlin, autopsies showed severe internal injury with little external sign ("Casper's sign"). Patients who look well may be dying. (N-ORT: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.otsr.2019.02.009; G-FOR: https://doi.org/10.1007/s12024-020-00277-6)
8. **Surge changes the surgical decision.** Nice surgeons judged non-operative management of abdominal injuries unsuitable during a mass influx and favoured surgical haemostasis. (N-VIS: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jviscsurg.2018.04.004)
9. **Bleeding control and blood logistics.** In Paris, tourniquet demand was so high that teams returned without their belts (P-HIR). Massive transfusion was needed in 6% of admissions, and the authors advise shortening the time to get blood to the bedside rather than growing the stockpile (P-TXF). (P-HIR: https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(15)01063-6; P-TXF: https://doi.org/10.1097/TA.0000000000002729)
10. **High-energy or fragmenting rounds need planned, repeated debridement.** At Utøya there were 101 operations in 4 weeks, mostly for progressive soft-tissue necrosis. (O-FRAG: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.injury.2016.03.023)
11. **Initial casualty counts underestimate.** In Barcelona the initial estimate of 20 rose to 153 victims (C-VAL). In Berlin the first triage round counted about half the final total, hence "plan for double" (G-JEMS). (C-VAL: https://revistaemergencias.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/131-139.pdf; G-JEMS: https://www.jems.com/?p=63633)
12. **Children and young people.** After Nice, 365 children and adolescents attended the paediatric medico-psychological emergency unit in the first 2 weeks (N-PSY). Utøya's surgical cohort had a median age of 17 (O-FRAG). Published paediatric physical-injury numbers for these attacks were **not found**. (N-PSY: https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2019.00065)
13. **Rehearsal pays off.** Pitié-Salpêtrière had run a Plan Blanc drill on the morning of the Paris attacks (P-PSL). Brussels credited familiarity between responders from joint exercises (B-MOH). (P-PSL: https://doi.org/10.1017/dmp.2016.21; B-MOH: https://doi.org/10.1017/S1049023X19001900)
14. **Smaller and remote hospitals can cope with planning and staff recall.** Ringerike, a rural hospital, triaged 35 patients and had no deaths (O-RUR). A remote Paris trauma centre received 53 victims in 4 h with only 3 diversions (P-REM). (O-RUR: https://doi.org/10.1002/bjs.9203; P-REM: https://doi.org/10.1097/MEJ.0000000000000555)
